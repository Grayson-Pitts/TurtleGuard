What is TurtleGuard?

TurtleGuard uses historical sea turtle nesting records and the TimesFM AI forecasting model to estimate nesting activity during the 2027 season.

We calculate predicted nests per mile to compare eight monitored Florida coastal areas. The resulting priorities can help conservation groups identify where additional monitoring, outreach and nest protection may be valuable.

Field guide / Species

Meet Florida's sea turtles.

Florida is a globally important sea turtle nesting region. Five species occur in its waters, and each faces distinct conservation challenges.

Loggerhead, green and leatherback turtles nest regularly in Florida, while hawksbill and Kemp's ridley nesting is uncommon. Learn where these species live, when they nest, and what makes their protection important. Seasons and nest sizes are approximate; legal statuses below refer to U.S. Endangered Species Act protection.

01 / Loggerhead Turtle

Caretta caretta

Federally threatened

Loggerheads are Florida's most common nesting sea turtle. Florida supports one of the largest loggerhead nesting populations in the world. Coastal development, fisheries interactions and artificial lighting remain important threats.

Where they are found
Both Atlantic and Gulf coasts. Major nesting areas include Brevard, Indian River, St. Lucie, Martin and Palm Beach counties.
Nesting season
Typically April through September, peaking in early summer.
Nesting behavior
Females generally lay several clutches per season, often containing approximately 100–125 eggs each.
Hatching
Incubation takes roughly 45–65 days. Hatchlings generally emerge at night.

02 / Green Sea Turtle

Chelonia mydas

Federally threatened — North Atlantic

Green sea turtle nesting in Florida has increased substantially following decades of protection. The species still faces marine debris, habitat degradation, vessel strikes and disease. Adults primarily feed on seagrass and algae.

Where they are found
Seagrass beds, coastal bays and reefs, with major nesting concentrations along Florida's Atlantic coast.
Nesting season
Primarily June through late September.
Nesting behavior
Females often return every two to three years and lay multiple clutches, averaging around 128 eggs per nest.
Hatching
Incubation generally lasts around 50–60 days. Hatchlings usually emerge at night.

03 / Leatherback Turtle

Dermochelys coriacea

Federally endangered

The leatherback is the largest living sea turtle. It travels across oceans, dives to remarkable depths and primarily feeds on jellyfish. Fishing gear entanglement and marine debris are major conservation concerns.

Where they are found
Open ocean habitats, nesting primarily along Florida's southeastern Atlantic coast, especially around Palm Beach County.
Nesting season
Primarily March through July in Florida, with occasional earlier or later nests.
Nesting behavior
Females return every few years and lay several nests per season, generally with about 70–90 yolked eggs.
Hatching
Incubation commonly takes about 60–70 days.

04 / Kemp's Ridley Turtle

Lepidochelys kempii

Federally endangered

Kemp's ridley is the smallest sea turtle species and one of the most endangered. It is famous for synchronized daytime nesting events called arribadas, which occur primarily in Mexico. Nesting in Florida is comparatively rare.

Where they are found
Shallow Gulf waters, estuaries and coastal waters around Florida.
Nesting season
Generally April through July.
Nesting behavior
Most nesting occurs in Mexico, though occasional nests are recorded on Florida beaches.
Hatching
Eggs generally incubate for 45–55 days.

05 / Hawksbill Turtle

Eretmochelys imbricata

Federally endangered

Hawksbills are closely associated with coral reef ecosystems. Their distinctive beak-like mouth helps them feed on reef organisms. The illegal shell trade, reef destruction and climate change have severely affected hawksbill populations.

Where they are found
Coral reefs and rocky habitats, especially in the Florida Keys and southeastern Florida.
Nesting season
Generally late spring through autumn where nesting occurs. Florida nesting is rare.
Nesting behavior
Florida nests are uncommon. Females may lay multiple clutches during a nesting season.
Hatching
Incubation generally lasts 50–70 days.

Field guide / Conservation

Small actions. Lasting impact.

Everyone can help protect Florida's sea turtles, whether they live near the coast, enjoy visiting beaches or support conservation from farther away.

01

Keep beachfront lighting turtle-friendly

Artificial lights can disorient nesting females and hatchlings, leading them away from the sea. During nesting and hatching season, turn off unnecessary beach-facing lights and close blinds. Essential lighting should be low-mounted, shielded and use turtle-friendly amber or red wavelengths. Avoid flash photography near nesting turtles.

02

Leave no trace on the beach

Fill in holes and flatten sandcastles before leaving. These obstacles can trap hatchlings and interfere with nesting females. Bring home umbrellas, beach chairs, toys and other equipment each evening.

03

Reduce plastic waste

Sea turtles can mistake floating plastics for prey or become entangled in discarded fishing line. Use reusable bags and bottles, properly dispose of waste, recycle fishing line and never release balloons outdoors.

04

Be responsible around turtles and marine life

Stay well away from nesting turtles and their nests. Never touch, chase or interfere with hatchlings. While boating, obey posted speed zones and watch carefully for wildlife, especially in shallow coastal waters and near inlets.

05

Get involved with a conservation organization

Join an organized beach cleanup, assist with conservation education, or apply to a trained nesting patrol or sea turtle rehabilitation program. Specialized turtle handling requires appropriate authorization and training.

Found an injured or stranded sea turtle?

Report sick, injured, entangled, stranded or dead sea turtles to the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission's 24-hour Wildlife Alert Hotline.

1-888-404-FWCC (3922)

Provide the animal's exact location and follow the responder's instructions. Do not handle the turtle or return it to the water yourself.
